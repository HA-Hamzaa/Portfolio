Taking two hours out of a Gulfstream G700 nacelle line

A four-week industrial internship in tooling methods. The line had a routing-time target to hit; I built a model that says where the time should come from, and how sure we can be that the plan holds.

01 — The problem

A target is a number. A plan says who gives up which minutes.

The G700 nacelle line had to go from 148 to 146 hours of routing time per nacelle. Two hours sounds small, but it has to be taken from 36 operations that do not have the same margin: some are already tight, others have slack, some are cheap to speed up and some are not.

The usual approach is to spread the cut evenly or by share of time. I wanted a split that respects what each operation can really give, and a way to check that the plan still holds when real times vary.

02 — Approach

From shop-floor data to a plan you can defend

I ran the model on the real routing data, where it found about 2.5 hours of potential reduction. Those data stay confidential, so the numbers on this page come from a synthetic replica with the same structure (36 operations, same order of magnitude of times).

  1. 1

    Map the operations

    36 operations, each with a time, a type, a criticality weight and a maximum reduction it can give. Run on the real routing; shown here with synthetic numbers.

  2. 2

    Write the allocation

    A convex quadratic program: minimize the weighted effort of reductions under the total-time target and per-operation bounds.

  3. 3

    Solve and compare

    A closed-form KKT solution (bounded water-filling), checked against a numerical solver, then compared with the spreadsheet rule of thumb.

  4. 4

    Stress-test it

    5,000 Monte Carlo runs with random variation on every operation time to measure how often the target is met.

  5. 5

    Turn it into actions

    Ranked list of operations to work on first, ready for the methods team.

03 — Results

Less effort than the rule of thumb, and robust down to 61 hours

On the synthetic line, 2.52 h have to come out of 64.52 h, out of 4.17 h that the operations could give at most. The model penalises large relative cuts, more strongly on critical operations:

min Σ Wi (gi / Ti)² subject to Σ gi = 2.52 h, 0 ≤ gi ≤ ḡi
The KKT conditions give each operation gi = min(ḡi, λ Ti² / 2Wi), with one scalar λ found by bisection. The numerical solver lands on the same answer to within 10−7 h.

The optimal split scores 0.1046 against 0.1312 for the rule of thumb: about 20% less weighted effort for the same two-and-a-half hours. It leans on long, non-critical operations, and ten of the 36 end up at their maximum reduction.

The Monte Carlo check perturbs every operation time and weight 5,000 times and asks whether the target is still reachable within the caps.

04 — Two checks on the model

Does the answer depend on my weights, and on cost?

Weights. The criticality weights are my judgement, so I rebuilt them five other ways (quality first, compressibility first, productivity first, and so on). The same five operations come out on top in five of the six schemes, and four of them in the last one.

Cost. Cutting time is not free, so I added a synthetic implementation cost per type of operation, weighted by α. Moving α from 0 to 0.2 lowers the implementation cost by 28% for 24% more reduction effort: the model shifts the cut toward operations that are cheap to change.

05 — Day to day

Tooling methods work on a live line

Next to the model, the internship was hands-on methods work for the line.

  • Handled tooling requests: specifications, CAD and follow-up with the shop floor.
  • Followed non-conformities and rated workstation postures on the line.
  • Reorganized the team's shared channel so requests and drawings were easier to find.
  • R&D on curing a shimming compound with radiant lamps instead of an 8-hour wait — later extended into a 3D thermal model (see the curing case study).
What I take away

An optimization is only useful on a shop floor if you can say how often it fails. The Monte Carlo step is what turned a nice result into a plan people could discuss.

What comes next
