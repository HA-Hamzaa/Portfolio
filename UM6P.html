A decision-support system for a university's IT equipment

How should an IT department forecast demand for laptops, screens, docks and adapters, and choose when to reorder, when every department behaves differently?

01 — The problem

Buying in a hurry is the most expensive way to manage stock.

The department managed IT equipment reactively: when something broke or ran out, it was bought. That meant urgent purchases at higher prices, rooms waiting for equipment, and surplus in one department while another ran short.

My supervisor gave me an open question: how could the department forecast demand and plan procurement, balancing availability, irregular demand, supplier lead times and cost? Most series are intermittent: months of zero, then a burst.

02 — What I built

A chain that turns raw files into a decision.

I kept prediction and decision separate on purpose. A good forecast is not enough: you still have to measure what a stock policy does to cost, stock-outs and urgent orders, then choose.

  1. 1

    Import and map

    Excel or CSV files from different sources, mapped to one canonical schema.

  2. 2

    Check quality

    Missing columns, negative stock, duplicates and impossible dates flagged before any model runs.

  3. 3

    Forecast demand

    Nine models, from naive to Croston, SBA and TSB, compared series by series with rolling-origin validation.

  4. 4

    Simulate policies

    Six policies, including (s,Q), (s,S) and minimum stock, run with lead times, safety stock and stock-outs.

  5. 5

    Recommend

    One policy per equipment line, plus a MILP layer designed for budget and transfers between departments.

03 — What the data said

No single model, no single policy.

04 — The prototype

Upload a file, read a recommendation.

A Python/FastAPI application: the team imports its files, checks the mapping, runs the forecasting and policy benchmarks, and exports the plan. I proposed it to the IT department, which adopted it.

Python FastAPI pandas Croston · SBA · TSB Inventory simulation MILP
05 — What I take away

“A model is not useful because it performs well mathematically. It has to represent the system, stay robust when assumptions change, and help someone make a better decision.”

What comes next

Calibrate costs, lead times and service targets on the department's representative history.

Connect the MILP layer, so budget, repairs and transfers between departments are decided together.
