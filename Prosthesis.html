Design project · Georges Charpak Institute · Sep 2026 — Present

A glenoid implant designed against loosening

In a five-member team working with the Georges Charpak Institute of Human Biomechanics, we study why shoulder implants loosen, propose a glenoid concept, measure real shoulder motion — and provoke loosening on a test bench to validate our models.

01 — The problem

The glenoid side is where implants fail

In an anatomic shoulder replacement, the humeral head is metal and the glenoid is a thin polyethylene cup cemented into a small bone. Before designing anything, we reviewed the evidence: four experimental studies and five finite-element studies, from Anglin (2000) to Bonnevialle (2022).

32%of total-shoulder complications are glenoid looseningBohsali et al., 2006
7%revision rate linked to glenoid looseningBohsali et al., 2006
14–33%occurrence of loosening across published seriesAbdul Wahab et al., 2017

How it fails

1Off-centre loadingThe humeral head translates toward the rim of the glenoid.
→
2Rocking horseThe opposite rim lifts off; micromotion starts at the interface.
→
3Interface fatigueCement microcracks, debris, bone loss — then loosening.

Why the uncemented central peg

Bone grows onto an implant only if it moves less than about 20 µm. Interface micromotion under eccentric load, from Abdul Wahab et al. (2016):

Fully cemented
21–24 µm
Finned peg, uncemented
6–12 µm
│ 20 µm bone-ingrowth threshold

Step 3 in detail

Measuring a real shoulder in 3D

An electromagnetic tracker follows two sensors, one on the flat of the acromion and one on the arm, while a stylus picks bony landmarks. From those points we build anatomical frames for the scapula and the humerus, then compute how the humerus rotates relative to the scapula during a 90° abduction.

  1. 1Palpate landmarks with the stylus: both humeral epicondyles and the acromion; on the scapula, the root of the spine, the acromial angle and the inferior angle.
  2. 2Build each anatomical frame from three points (midpoints, normalised vectors, cross products) as a rotation matrix.
  3. 3Calibrate: link each anatomical frame to its sensor at t = 0, so the sensors can carry the frames during motion.
  4. 4Chain the matrices at every sample and decompose the result into three joint angles.
Rscap→hum(t) = Rscap→s1 · Rs1→s2(t) · Rs2→hum
calibrated at t = 0 · tracked · calibrated at t = 0

Set-up rules from our protocol: emitter within 80 cm of the sensors, subject away from metal, slow movement, elbow at 90° to start, three repetitions.

Honest reading. Repeatability within the session looks good qualitatively, especially for a1 and a3. Quantifying it would need each cycle time-normalised to 0–100%; with a single session, between-session repeatability is not assessed.
04 — Bench test

Making an implant loosen, on purpose

To validate the finite-element model, we provoked the failure we want to prevent. A 3D-printed glenoid implant with a 16 × 6 mm keel was cemented in a block of polyethylene bone foam, then loaded on an Instron 5565 until it rocked loose.

I prepared the specimen: the foam surface milled to the implant's spherical back, a 17 × 7 mm slot dug for the keel to leave 0.5 mm of PMMA cement around it — the thin end of the literature range, which loads the implant–cement interface hardest.

Choosing where to push

The machine has a single actuator, so it cannot alternate between the two rims like a humeral head does. The implant's double symmetry leaves three candidate points; only one, beyond the tapered end of the keel, lets the implant tip over. We loaded there, 5 mm from the rim, normal to the articular surface:

α = arcsin(d / R) = arcsin(10 / 27) ≈ 21.8°

Protocol

  1. 1 · Static reference10 mm/min up to 750 N, safety stop at 2 mm.
  2. 2 · 900 load cyclesBetween about 47 and 760 N.
  3. 3 · Static controlSame as step 1: the difference is the loosening.
A Basler camera tracks a dot on the implant and one on the cement at 5 frames/s (≈ 0.024 mm per pixel); movements under the 0.02 mm noise are ignored.

Result: clean loosening in under 1,000 cycles

TestMax loadImplant lift vs cementLoosening
Static reference746 N0.02 mmNo — noise level
Static control, after 900 cycles741 N1.97 mmYes
Complementary test1,312 N2.58 → 4.53 mmInterface destroyed

The machine curves agree: after cycling, the assembly needs 0.4 mm of slack before it carries load again, and about 0.8 mm of permanent set built up during the cycles — a third of it in the first 100.

Limits we report One actuator, so no rim-to-rim alternation; homogeneous foam instead of cortical and cancellous bone; the cycles were not filmed, so we do not know when lift-off began.
05 — Finite-element model

Matching a test in Abaqus: the supports decide

Before modelling the implant in bone, the model has to reproduce the implant alone. The reference: a keeled glenoid printed in Zortrax Resin Basic, resting by its rim on an aluminium block and pushed on the keel by an aluminium cylinder up to 200 N at 1 mm/min. A camera tracks a black dot on the short side: 0.228 mm at 150 N.

I imported the CAD part into Abaqus, modelled it as linear-elastic, ramped the load on the keel face, and replaced the cylinder and the block by a load and boundary conditions. This was an individual assignment within the project.

Six attempts, one lesson

Fixing two vertices let the part rotate: 0.45 mm, twice the test. Fixing the whole rim made it far too stiff: 0.045 mm. The truth sat between, so I cut short contact arcs on the rim and made their length a parameter. Supports moved the answer by a factor of 10; the mesh by less than 6%.

Mistakes that taught me something

A measuring point that moved. I first read the displacement on a mesh node; after remeshing, the node changed. The dot is now a geometric vertex, and the output request is rebuilt after each remesh.

Constraints that did nothing. Solid elements have no rotational degrees of freedom, so the rotation boxes I ticked were inactive — Abaqus warned about it. A run that finishes is not a model that is right.

Closing the gap

The best model lands 2% from the test. But material, supports and mesh changed together, so I report it as a calibration, not a validation.

To call it validated Export exact XY data instead of reading curves; redo the mesh study on the final supports (0.6, 0.5, 0.4 mm); change one parameter at a time; check against the 200 N point and the crosshead curve as well.
06 — Materials

What is a real implant made of?

In a polymer characterisation lab, we identified the material of a commercial prosthesis component, then worked back to how it was made.

FTIR spectroscopy A linear polyethylene Only C–H bands (2915, 2848, ≈1470, ≈720 cm⁻¹): no carbonyl, hydroxyl, aromatic or C–F groups, so not PMMA, PA, PET or PTFE. A weak CH₃ band rules out branched low-density PE.
DSC, 10 °C/min
1st heat (as made)Tm 138.7 °C · 43% crystalline
2nd heatTm 136.6 °C · 55% crystalline
Melting at 137–139 °C means linear PE; the low as-made crystallinity and a melt that would not flow point to UHMWPE.
Back to the process Sintered, then machined UHMWPE cannot be injected: it is compression-sintered, machined, and sterilised without oxygen — gamma in air would have left a C=O band. The embedded metal wire is a radiographic marker for orientation, wear and migration.
Same module · composite infusion

We also chose a cure cycle for an infusion epoxy from DSC (reaction enthalpy 327 J/g, peak at 112.6 °C), modelled Tg against conversion with the Di Benedetto law, infused a 4-ply plate under vacuum, and estimated its permeability from the resin front with Darcy's law: K ≈ 4.7 × 10⁻¹² m².

Our measured network Tg (42.7 °C) was far below the datasheet's 129 °C; we traced it to a 312 mg mix weighed on a 10 mg-resolution balance, and recommend mixing a few grams.

What I take away

Medical design is mostly about evidence. The most useful test was the one built to fail, and the most useful model was the one that first got it wrong: in both, the boundary conditions mattered more than the details.
